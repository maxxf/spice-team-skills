# The Spice Client Email Standard

Every rule on this page came out of 31 emails Spice actually sent to clients and
prospects in the last 120 days. No adjectives, no vibes. If a rule is here, a
real email failed it, and that email is cited.

Read this before you write to a client. It sits next to
`maxx-freedman-voice-guide.md`: the voice guide tells you how a Spice sentence
sounds, this page tells you what shape the email is.

---

## The skeleton

Five slots, in this order. Most client emails are done in five.

1. **Headline outcome.** Name, then the thing that happened or the thing you
   need. First sentence carries it.
2. **The number or the change.** One or two figures that prove the headline.
   Not the whole table.
3. **The one thing the client must decide or do.** Singular where possible.
   This lands in the first three lines or you have already lost.
4. **The link to the artifact.** The analysis, the calendar, the invoice, the
   prototype. The email points at it. The email is not it.
5. **Sign off.** Your name. Optionally one line of specific human attention
   (see below), which is the part nobody skips.

The order is not decorative. Slots 1 through 3 are what gets read on a phone in
a parking lot. Slot 4 is what gets opened at a desk. If your ask is in slot 4,
it does not exist.

---

## The ceiling: 150 words

**A composed client email tops out at 150 words. Past that, the overflow becomes
a link.**

- **Hard stop at 200.** Between 150 and 200 is a warning that something in
  there belongs in a linked artifact. Above 200 is a fail regardless of how
  good the content is.
- **Replies under 40 words are exempt.** Half of what Spice sends is a
  one-liner, that is healthy, and a ceiling on "Heard, launching Thursday" is
  noise.

Why 150 and not something rounder: 81% of the current sample already clears it.
This codifies what most of the team already does rather than importing a target
from nowhere. The six emails it excludes are the exact six this standard exists
to fix. Setting the line at the p90 (195 words) would ratify the sprawl. Setting
it at 100 would break emails that legitimately carry three decisions.

The point of the number is not tidiness. At 150 you cannot both link the
analysis and retype it, so the ceiling forces the structural choice: restate or
link. Trim adjectives and you will still fail. Move a section into the doc and
you pass.

---

## Put the ask in the first three lines

This is the rule the sample most badly needs.

15 of 31 emails put the ask in the first three lines. Among the nine long-form
emails, exactly one does. The longer a Spice email gets, the further down the
ask slides. The 466-word Westville email buries its only real request, approval
to launch an A/B test, in item three of four, roughly 300 words in.

So the rule has a corollary: **if you have four items, lead with the one that
needs a decision, not the one that happened first.** Chronology is for the
linked doc. The email is ordered by what you need back.

---

## Link, don't restate

Spice already links. 13 emails in the sample cite specific numbers and 10 of
them link a source doc. Among long-form emails citing numbers, six of seven link
out. The habit is close to universal.

The failure is what the email says *after* the link. The Westville email links a
full breakdown and then reproduces the breakdown above the link. Harol's
campaign calendar links the calendar, then restates two of its entries.

Linking is not the missing discipline. **Trusting the link is.** Pull one number
out of the doc, the one that makes your point, and let the doc carry the rest.
If you find yourself writing "full breakdown below" after having written the
full breakdown, delete the breakdown, not the link.

## One line of specific human attention, every email

Every client email carries at least one line that only a person who looked at
this account this week could have written. A detail, a reaction, a callback to
something they said on the call, an oddity in their numbers you noticed and have
not fully explained yet.

Examples from the sample, all real:

- "Eitan, to your point on the call, we're still isolating how many UES
  customers were genuinely new to the brand."
- "ROAS got worse anyway, 14.4x to 9.1x. Efficiency dropping on a smaller
  budget is unusual."
- "Odds are it just slipped through."

This is the load-bearing rule on the page, and it is the one that makes the
ceiling safe. A short email with nothing human in it does not read as
respectful of the client's time. It reads as disengaged, like the account is on
autopilot and you are clearing a queue. Brevity plus one specific observation
reads as "I know this account well enough to be quick." Brevity alone reads as
"I have not thought about you since the last one of these."

If you cannot write the line, that is information. You have not looked at the
account closely enough to be sending an update yet.

## The skeleton against the five formats

Same five slots, five different jobs. Every example below is under the ceiling.

> Hannah, Jamie,

>
> Creative draft lands July 10, and we need sign-off that same day or the July 14
> launch slips a week.
>
> Two other things closed out: store naming is reconciled across DoorDash and

> Tampa/New Tampa mismatches. The $5 review reward is off everywhere except

The voice guide's kill list still applies on top of this one. That list is about
word choice. This list is about the specific padding Spice reaches for when
writing to clients.

**Three patterns worth keeping**, for contrast. These are in the sample and are
house voice working correctly:

- "Short version:" / "Quick version:" (maxx)
- "Odds are it just slipped through." (finance)
- The bare-name direct opener, "Kaveh," or "Shai," (maxx, 14 instances)

One calibration note. The single worst opener in the whole mailbox, "I hope this
finds you thriving," was written by a client to us, not by us. Nobody at Spice
wrote anything like it. **Our tells are structural, not epistolary.** We do not
have a flowery-greeting problem. We have a length-and-buried-ask problem, which
is harder to see and easier to fix.

## Where this standard comes from, and what it does not prove

Read this part before you cite this page at somebody.

**The sample.** 31 emails sent to a client, prospect, or client-side contact
over 120 days, covering 18 organizations and six senders. Median 57 words, p90
195, longest 466.

**Its limits.** One mailbox, Maxx's, not the team's. Teammate emails show up
only where he was a recipient or CC, which biases toward threads he was already
on and undersamples ops-analyst-to-client traffic. The counts for Daniel, Harol,
and Manish are individually too small to be conclusive. Treat per-sender numbers
as indicative.

**The drift is concentrated, not team-wide.** Maxx's 22 emails have a median of
41 words and 19 of them are under 140. The long, structurally AI-shaped emails
come from the teammates who write *composed* client updates rather than replies:
recaps, onboarding sequences, campaign calendars. Ad-hoc replies across the whole
team are fine. This is a tooling problem, not a talent problem, which is why the
fix is a shared structure the skills emit rather than a conversation with
individuals.

**Where this came from.** A client told us our email read as machine-generated.
Shai Lochoff at Westville, 2026-08-05, replying to the 466-word email above:
"These are VERY AI looking."

The sample explains what he was reacting to. That email was the longest of the
31. It buried its only real ask 300 words down, at item three of four. And it
linked a full analysis and then retyped the analysis directly above the link.
That is what AI-shaped writing looks like from the other side of the inbox. Not
a vocabulary problem. A shape problem. Every rule on this page exists to make
that shape hard to produce by accident.

## Before you hit send

- [ ] Under 150 words? (Replies under 40 are exempt. Over 200 is a fail.)
- [ ] Is the ask in the first three lines?
- [ ] Did you link the artifact instead of retyping it?
- [ ] Is there one line only someone who looked at this account could write?
- [ ] Zero banned patterns from the table above?
- [ ] Zero em dashes, per the voice guide?
- [ ] One closing question, not three soft exits?

If the email fails on length, do not trim adjectives. Find the paragraph that is
duplicating the linked doc and delete it. That is almost always where the extra
words are.
